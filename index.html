How it works, in plain words

Like your phone's next-word suggestion, scaled up.

It predicts words

It learned from huge amounts of text. It does not look facts up unless it searches the web.

Confident does not mean correct

It can state a wrong number in the same tone as a right one (this is called hallucinating).

Its knowledge stops at a date

Today's rates, rules, and prices may be missing unless it searches or you tell it.

It only knows what you tell it

It cannot see your accounts, your credit report, or your mail.

Stop and get a person when

Money is about to move today. Someone is rushing you or asking for secrecy. It needs account numbers or passwords. You are filing taxes or signing a legal paper. The answer changes a big decision.

The tools

Each has a free version. Pick one and learn it well. ChatGPT (OpenAI): all-purpose, voice conversations. Gemini (Google): works with Gmail and Google search. Copilot (Microsoft): built into Windows, Edge, Word, and Outlook. Claude (Anthropic): long documents and careful explanations. Meta AI: inside WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram. Your bank's app: many banks have a built-in assistant inside their secure app.

Five rules before you type

They do not change from one assistant to another.

1

Nothing that identifies you.

No account numbers, passwords, Social Security numbers, or your full name and address. Round numbers are enough for the assistant; keep the exact figures on your own list.
2

Give it the date and your real numbers.

It does not know today's interest rates, your bank's rules, or your credit report. Tell it.
3

Ask for options and their risks.

"Give me three ways to handle this and the risk of each" beats "what should I do."
4

Verify one fact before you act.

A loan calculator, the source document, or a phone call. One check catches most wrong answers.
5

When money is about to move, a human confirms first.

Your bank, a nonprofit credit counselor, your spouse, or a trusted advisor.

How to ask

Brief it like a great new hire. It works hard, but it only knows what you tell it.

1

Role

"You are a patient budgeting coach."
2

Context

"I am retired. My income is about $2,400 a month."
3

Task

"Help me plan holiday spending under $500."
4

Boundaries

"Do not suggest new credit. Tell me if you are unsure."
5

Format

"A short list in plain words."

Then iterate: tell it what worked, what missed, and go again.

Six ways to get a better answer

Ask where it got that. "What is your source? Give me the link."
Allow "I don't know." "If you are not sure, say so."
Ask it to show the math. "Show each step so I can check it."
Ask what could be wrong. "What might you be wrong about here?"
Correct it. "My rent is $1,250, not $1,500. Redo it."
Start a new chat for a new topic, so old details do not mix in.

The ASK method

Three moves, in this order, for any money question. "Test all things; hold fast what is good." 1 Thessalonians 5:21

A  Anchor

Give the facts, the numbers, and your boundaries: what you will not touch (rent, tithe, emergency fund) and your deadline.

S  Seek

Ask for three options, the risk of each, and the questions you should ask before you decide. Options are the goal.

K  Keep testing

Verify one number with an independent source. Ask "what could you be wrong about?" Take the result to a person.

Prompt cards

Tap Copy, paste into your assistant, then change the numbers in brackets to your own.

1. The holiday plan

I have [$600] total for gifts, food, and travel between today and January 1. I need gifts for [9] people ([2 parents, 3 children, 2 friends, 2 coworkers]). I am hosting [one dinner for 8] and attending [two gatherings]. Build a plan that stays under my total, show me where I am most likely to overspend, and give me two ways to cut $100 if I need to.

Keep testing: add up the plan yourself. Does it land under your number?

2. The car offer

A dealer is offering me an [$18,500] car with [$0] down, [72] months at [12%] APR, plus a [$1,800] extended warranty rolled into the loan. My take-home pay is [$3,400] a month and my rent is [$1,250]. Give me the monthly payment, the total I would pay over the life of the loan, three risks in this deal, and five questions to ask before I sign anything.

Keep testing: run $20,300 ($18,500 car plus the $1,800 warranty) at 12% for 72 months in the loan calculator. Expected: about $397 a month, about $28,600 total. Follow-up to ask: "What changes if I skip the warranty?"

3. The letter

Explain this letter to me in plain English. What is it asking me to do, by what date, and what happens if I do nothing? What three questions should I ask when I call the number on it? [Paste the text of the letter here, or attach a photo with your name, address, and account number covered.]

Keep testing: call the number printed on the letter itself, never one that arrives by text. A sample letter for practice is below.

4. The message

Is this message likely a scam? List the signs and tell me what to do. Message: "Hi Mom, this is my new number. I am in trouble and need $400 on a gift card tonight. Please do not call, I cannot talk right now."

Keep testing: urgency plus secrecy plus gift cards or codes equals a scam. Hang up and call the person on the number you already have.

5. The statement (this week)

Explain this to me in plain English: [paste one line from a pay stub, a 401(k) or retirement plan notice, or a savings account statement, with your name and account number removed]. What does it mean for me, what is one question I should ask my HR office or my bank, and what would change if I moved [$50] a month toward it?

Keep testing: take the one question to the person who administers the account.

6. Time on your side (this week)

I am [___] years old. Show me, in a simple table, what [$50] a month becomes by age 65 at 4 percent, 7 percent, and 10 percent a year. Then show me the Rule of 72 for each rate: how many years until the money doubles, and how many doublings I have left. Explain it the way you would to a friend.

Keep testing: check one row in a compound interest calculator (investor.gov, from the SEC).

Your turn: the template

Here is my situation: [the facts, in round numbers, and what I will not touch]. I am deciding whether to [the decision] by [when]. Give me three options, the risk of each, the questions I should ask before deciding, and what you might be wrong about.

Keep testing: write down the one fact you will verify tomorrow and the one person you will bring the result to.

Sample letter (fictional)

NORTHGATE RECOVERY SERVICES, LLC P.O. Box 4410, Wilmington, DE 19899 Date: January 11, 2027 Reference: NRS-2261-0937 Dear Consumer, Northgate Recovery Services, LLC is a debt collector. We are trying to collect a debt that you owe to Delaware Valley Medical Associates. We will use any information you give us to help collect the debt. Our information shows: You had an account with Delaware Valley Medical Associates for services on March 14, 2026. As of December 31, 2026, you owed $1,284.50. Between December 31, 2026 and today, you were charged $0.00 in interest and $0.00 in fees, and you paid $0.00. Total amount of the debt now: $1,284.50. How can you dispute the debt? Call or write to us by February 15, 2027 to dispute all or part of the debt. If you do not, we will assume that our information is correct. If you write to us by February 15, 2027, we must stop collection on any amount you dispute until we send you information that shows you owe the debt. What else can you do? Write to ask for the name and address of the original creditor. Go to www.cfpb.gov/debt-collection to learn more about your rights. Contact us: (302) 555-0148, Monday to Friday, 9 am to 6 pm ET.

Modeled on the validation notice format collectors are required to send. What to notice: the dispute deadline, the amount, the original creditor, and the sentence about what happens if you do nothing.

Know your rights

Before you sign a loan: the lender must show the APR and total finance charge in writing (Truth in Lending Act).
Debt collector: must send a written notice; you get at least 30 days to dispute (Fair Debt Collection Practices Act).
Credit card error: dispute in writing within 60 days of the statement (Fair Credit Billing Act).
Identity theft worry: a credit freeze at all three bureaus is free.
Buying a car: there is generally no 3-day right to cancel once you sign at the dealer.
General information, not legal advice.

Free nonprofit credit counseling

nfcc.org or (800) 388-2227. Budget review, debt plans, housing counseling.

Your credit report, free

annualcreditreport.com. The only site authorized by federal law. Weekly access from all three bureaus.

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau

consumerfinance.gov. Plain-language guides on debt collection, auto loans, and credit. File a complaint about a company.

Report a scam

reportfraud.ftc.gov. For bank alerts: call the number on the back of your card. For a relative in trouble: call them on the number you already have.
